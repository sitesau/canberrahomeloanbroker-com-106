The commission model behind a no-fee service

Understanding how brokers get paid explains why the service is usually free. The source material sets out the structure clearly: an upfront commission, typically 0.5 to 0.7 per cent of the loan value, plus a trailing commission of around 0.15 to 0.2 per cent annually, both paid by the lender rather than the borrower.

This model has a practical implication for comparisons. Because the broker is paid by the lender at settlement, the cost is embedded in the loan product rather than charged as a separate invoice. Moneysmart recommends comparing home loans on fees, features and suitability as well as interest rate, which is a useful discipline: the right question is not what the broker charges, but whether the recommended loan's overall cost and features beat the alternatives once every factor is weighed together.

The costs that sit beyond the broker fee

The broader cost picture extends well past the broker fee. Several concrete markers in the source material matter here. Lenders mortgage insurance can be avoided through the First Home Guarantee, which allows a purchase with a 5 per cent deposit without lenders mortgage insurance, while Help to Buy can allow a 2 per cent deposit. Without a government scheme, a 20 per cent deposit avoids lenders mortgage insurance.

Stamp duty is the other large figure. From July 2025, the source material states that eligible buyers pay no stamp duty on properties up to $1,020,000 under the ACT Home Buyer Concession Scheme, with concessional rates up to $1,455,000 and a maximum saving of $35,238. Applicants must not have owned property in Australia in the past five years and must live in the property for at least one year, so eligibility is a decision gate, not an afterthought.

Refinancers face a different test. The material notes that savings should be calculated after exit costs, not before them, because a sharper rate only helps if the switch still stacks up once discharge costs and settlement time are counted. Construction borrowers have their own cost pattern, with progressive drawdown loans releasing funds at each building stage, which makes timing and builder paperwork part of the cost picture.

How to compare the true cost of a loan

A disciplined comparison weighs approval fit, fees, features and timing together. The source material suggests asking which lenders fit your scenario rather than chasing the cheapest advertised rate, and checking the trade-off between a lower rate and features such as an offset account.

  • Approval fit first. Ask which lenders are realistic for your file and which are poor fits before comparing rates.
  • Features as a bundle. Compare rate, offset access, redraw and flexibility together, not one by one.
  • Refinance maths. Ask how savings are tested after exit costs rather than before them.
  • Structure risk. Ask whether the loan still works if your plans change after purchase.

Anyone weighing mortgage broker canberra across providers should apply this broader frame rather than relying on headline rates alone.
